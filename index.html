Featured study Survey design SPSS Qualtrics

Social media & college students’ real-life confidence

PSY 493: Survey Research Methods · Michigan State University · Aug–Dec 2025 · with A. Keifer & H. Seymour

A cross-sectional survey of MSU students recruited through SONA and run in Qualtrics. We built a multi-construct instrument from validated scales (Social Media Attitude Scale, Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale, Social Skills Inventory, Social Self-Efficacy Scale) plus new items, then analyzed it end-to-end in SPSS.

  1. What are students’ attitudes toward social media?
  2. When did they start, and how has use changed into college?
  3. Do some groups start earlier or use it more? (ANOVA, Welch’s t)
  4. Is usage related to social skills and face-to-face confidence? (Spearman’s ρ)
My role & the full findings

My role

  • Co-designed the research questions and the survey instrument, combining four validated scales with new items on start age and usage.
  • Built the survey in Qualtrics and recruited participants through SONA (target 150; 211 completed).
  • Ran the end-to-end analysis in SPSS: descriptives, assumption checks (Shapiro–Wilk, Levene’s), one-way ANOVA, Welch’s t-tests, and Spearman correlations.
  • Co-wrote the 40-page report and presented the findings.

What we found

  • Confidence (RQ1): 38.9% felt equally confident online and offline; 33.5% felt slightly more confident on social media. 60.1% said they have more of a presence in real life than online.
  • Start age (RQ2): students started at about 12.4 years on average (range 7–19) and got their first phone at about 12.2. They rated 14.3 as the right age to start, and 46.8% wished they had started at a different age, almost all of them later.
  • Usage (RQ2): about 4.1 hours a day in college and 6.4 days a week in high school. 83.8% scroll “often” or “almost always” in their free time, though problematic-use scores stayed low on average (BSMAS M = 2.48 of 5).
  • Group differences (RQ3): no significant differences in daily use by gender or race/ethnicity, or in start age by family financial situation.
  • Social functioning (RQ4): social skills tracked strongly with extraversion (ρ = .63) and real-life confidence (ρ = .59), but social media use showed no meaningful correlation with either.

Recommendations we made

  • Digital-wellness workshops that help students reflect on early adoption and build healthier habits.
  • First-year programs that build real-life social confidence directly, since social media neither helps nor harms it on its own.
  • Future research comparing passive scrolling with active posting, and following early adopters longitudinally.

What’s going on?

Reading is so automatic for fluent readers that the word competes with the ink color you’re asked to name. When they clash, you slow down. This is called interference.

First described by J. Ridley Stroop in 1935, it’s one of the most replicated effects in psychology and a standard measure of attention and cognitive control.

I designed this demo the way I’d design a real study: a balanced mix of matching and clashing trials, randomized order, and only correct responses counted toward reaction time.
